# Style Grammar

This reference records the visual decisions that recur across the 25 source artworks. Use it to calibrate judgment, not to force every image into one formula.

## 1. The shared proposition

The quartet is a compact visual essay about one scene. Reality supplies evidence; abstraction returns its rhythm, silhouette, interval, or emotional residue. The result is quiet, editorial, and materially tactile.

The source set includes architecture, flowers, landscape, animals, foliage, vehicles, reflections, and monochrome facades. Subject matter changes; the transferable grammar does not.

## 2. Canvas and grid

- Cell aspect ratio: every individual cell is exactly 2:3 portrait.
- Final aspect ratio: the assembled quartet is also exactly 2:3 portrait.
- Core structure: four rectangular cells in two columns and two rows.
- Required grid: four equal 2:3 quadrants with almost invisible gutters. Do not vary cell widths or heights, because that would break the per-cell ratio invariant.
- Outer margins: none or very small. The pale field should feel continuous through adjacent abstract cells.
- Divider treatment: hairline ivory, pale gray, or a seam created by value change; avoid thick black borders.

Useful layout logics:

- **Diagonal counterweight:** strongest photograph at upper left; strongest abstraction at lower right.
- **Representational top / abstract bottom:** useful when the source has a clear horizon, facade, or botanical subject.
- **Alternating evidence:** photographic panels on a diagonal, abstractions on the opposite diagonal.
- **Light-to-dark circulation:** place the darkest cells diagonally so one half does not become visually heavy.

## 3. Fixed transformation sequence

The quartet should contain four fixed transformations in reading order, not four media filters: unchanged source crop, magnified abstract detail, minimal structural reduction, and dreamlike optical afterimage.

The three generated distances must separate by mechanism. The second changes scale and isolates a source detail until it becomes a monumental field; the third reduces relationships to a sparse score; the fourth changes time, light, or optical continuity into a dream state. If all three reuse the same silhouette, brush language, density, or centered composition, the quartet is visually redundant even when each cell is attractive.

The supplied style example is not a palette or subject template. Its reusable value is the contrast between close/magnified, sparse/exact, and light/dream. Rebuild those functions from the new source rather than carrying over flowers, pink-blue color, petal membranes, stamens, or prismatic details.

### Evidence

Retain the scene's factual identity through a crop, recognizable lighting, or faithful photographic texture. One strong crop is better than a full-scene thumbnail when the original is busy.

### Magnified Abstraction

Choose one source-specific detail—edge, gap, reflection, texture, fold, material junction, or color boundary—and enlarge it until it behaves like an abstract field. Preserve the local evidence while removing the complete-object view.

### Minimal Structure

Translate spatial relations rather than objects. Examples: horizon becomes bands; reflection becomes vertical drips; railings become repeated slats; pagoda tiers become centered bars; branching becomes sparse vectors; road curves become broad arcs.

Minimal means fewer marks, not a disconnected palette. Keep the lower-left inside the quartet's shared field family and reuse at least two locked color roles from the source. If its flatness makes it read as a separate poster, preserve the geometry and bring its background cast, value span, and accent color back into alignment with the other cells.

### Dream Afterimage

Return atmosphere without restoring all detail. Use source-supported optical bloom, refraction, bokeh, stretched light, veils, ghosted repetition, mirrored drift, or a fragment dissolving into space. Dreamlike means altered light and time, not imported fantasy symbols.

## 4. Source-derived visual DNA

Choose two to four pieces of DNA before generation:

- dominant contour or axis;
- characteristic interval or repetition;
- positive/negative-space ratio;
- darkest value location;
- one material cue such as fog, rain, glass, stone, foliage, grain, or bokeh;
- one muted hue and one accent hue.

Repeat DNA across panels in altered form. For example, a tower's centerline may reappear as a fine orange stroke; a blossom cluster may become translucent disks; a curved facade may return as intersecting arcs.

## 5. Color behavior

Source fidelity includes color energy. Preserve the photograph's actual value range, saturation, temperature contrast, and translucency before choosing any material treatment. Do not automatically turn a luminous or vivid photograph into muted paper tones; restraint should come from editing and spatial clarity, not compulsory desaturation.

Preferred base fields include warm ivory, bone, parchment, fog gray, muted sage, charcoal, and near-black. Sample accents from the source, then soften or deepen them so the quartet remains coherent.

Typical palette patterns:

- ivory + charcoal + one ochre/red accent;
- ivory + sage/forest green + blush;
- fog blue + ink gray + faded orange;
- near-black + botanical green + luminous amber;
- monochrome concrete values + one rust or gold pin.

Do not add a fashionable accent that has no relationship to the source. Avoid rainbow balancing, candy gradients, neon outlines, and four unrelated quadrant palettes.

For dependable cohesion, lock a shared palette bridge before generation: one dominant field family, one dark anchor, and one or two source accents. All generated cells use these roles in different proportions. The lower-left must visibly share a field or accent with its neighbors and must never be the quartet's only off-temperature or off-saturation cell.

## 6. Surface and mark

The common surface language combines photographic softness with print and paper cues:

- faint paper grain, dust, fibers, or dry-brush breakup;
- translucent watercolor-like fields;
- soft-edged pigment clouds or ink diffusion;
- precise but sparse vector lines;
- low-contrast graphite or charcoal shading;
- bokeh, bloom, mist, or slow-shutter traces when supported by the source.

Use texture as atmosphere, not as a universal overlay. Keep at least one large quiet area free of visible effects.

## 7. Typography

Typography is optional and subordinate. When used:

- one brief phrase, often two to five English words;
- tiny uppercase serif or restrained editorial grotesk;
- generous tracking and substantial surrounding space;
- optional date, index, or micro-caption at one edge;
- black, warm gray, or a sampled accent at low visual weight.

Do not turn the poster into an advertisement. Avoid large centered slogans, multiple font families, faux magazine mastheads, long paragraphs, watermarks, and meaningless pseudo-English.

## 8. Tension pairs to balance

The best results hold several tensions at once:

- real / remembered;
- detailed / sparse;
- hard geometry / soft atmosphere;
- dark anchor / pale field;
- repetition / interruption;
- object / interval;
- photographic depth / paper flatness.

Do not resolve all four panels toward the same side of a tension pair.

## 9. Failure conditions

Reject or revise when:

- the output reads as four unrelated pictures;
- every panel is a filtered copy of the source;
- the grid becomes a scrapbook with heavy borders or drop shadows;
- the source motif disappears completely from three abstract cells;
- detail density is uniform across all panels;
- empty space is filled with decorative symbols without source evidence;
- color is decorative rather than source-derived;
- the composition is a 1x4 strip, four-panel vertical sequence, or ambiguous mosaic instead of a 2x2 quartet;
- any individual cell is not 2:3, or the assembled quartet is not 2:3;
- text becomes the focal point or contains artifacts.

## 10. Prompt scaffold

Adapt this logic to the specific photograph instead of copying it verbatim:

> Create four equal 2:3 portrait cells and assemble them as one vertical 2:3 editorial 2x2 quartet derived from the attached photograph. Preserve [factual anchor] as recognizable evidence in one cell. Carry [motif/axis] through the other cells as isolated essence, structural geometry, and atmospheric afterimage. Use [source palette] on [paper field], hairline seams, generous negative space, [supported surface cues], and only [typography decision]. Balance dark/light and dense/quiet diagonally. The four cells must feel like one visual essay, with no heavy borders, unrelated imagery, stock decoration, or illegible text.

The final prompt should name the source-specific evidence and transformations explicitly.
